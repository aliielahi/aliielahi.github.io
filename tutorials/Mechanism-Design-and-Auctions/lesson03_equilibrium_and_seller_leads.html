Lesson 3: Equilibrium of bidding functions, and the seller who leads over it

About 65 minutes. Prerequisites: Lesson 1 (reaction functions, 2.6) and Lesson 2 (mechanisms, DSIC). Three figures, a quiz at the end. Every number here was checked by simulation.

Where this lesson goes

In Lesson 2 the seller could predict the bids because the mechanism was truthful: bid = value. GSP and first price are not truthful, so that prediction fails. We need two things:

  1. A prediction of what bidders do when the mechanism is not truthful. That is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium (BNE) of bidding functions. We derive one by hand (3.2), then learn to compute one numerically inside a restricted class, the way you will for MIRT-GSP and NGA (3.3).
  2. A way for the seller to choose its design knowing bidders will move to that equilibrium. This is a Stackelberg game with the seller leading, and the follower's "reaction" is the whole equilibrium (3.5).

Along the way: what GSP's equilibria look like, and why GSP's extra revenue at truthful bids can disappear (3.4); and why uniform shading changes MIR's allocation but not a rank-by-bid allocation (3.6). We end with the recipe for the equilibrium engine (3.7).

3.1 From Nash to Bayes–Nash: what a strategy is when you only know your own value (8 min)

A Nash equilibrium (Lesson 1, 1.3) needs each player to best-respond to the others' actions. In an auction, what a bidder can respond to depends on what it knows. There are two models:

Full informationIncomplete information (Bayesian)
Bidder knowseveryone's valuesits own value \(v_i\), and the distribution \(F\) of the others' values
A strategy isone bid \(b_i\)a bidding function \(\beta_i: v\mapsto b\), one bid for every value it might have
EquilibriumNash: no bidder gains by changing its bidBayes–Nash: no bidder, at any value, gains by changing its bid, on average over the others' values
In your workbalanced bidding in NEK_GSPwhat a real advertiser (or the Stackelberg paper's leader) can do
Bayes–Nash equilibrium. Bidding functions \((\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n)\) form a BNE if, for every bidder \(i\) and every value \(v\), \[ \beta_i(v)\in\arg\max_{b}\ \ \mathbb{E}_{V_{-i}\sim F}\Big[\,u_i\big(v;\ b,\ \beta_{-i}(V_{-i})\big)\Big]. \] The expectation is the key difference from Nash: the bidder does not see the others' bids, only their distribution. A BNE is symmetric if all bidders use the same \(\beta\).
Why this matters for NEK_GSP Balanced bidding lets each advertiser react to the exact bids it faces, which no real advertiser sees. A full-information equilibrium is therefore the most a bidder could squeeze out, an upper bound on strategic gains. A BNE strategy has to work on average over situations it cannot tell apart, which is the realistic model. It is also what the Stackelberg papers use: their bidders commit to functions \(\beta\).

3.2 Deriving the first-price equilibrium (12 min)

Setup. One item, \(n\) bidders, values i.i.d. \(U[0,1]\). The highest bid wins and pays its own bid. We look for a symmetric BNE \(\beta\) that is increasing with \(\beta(0)=0\).

Step 1. Write one bidder's problem

Fix bidder 1 with value \(v\) and suppose the others bid \(\beta(V_j)\). If bidder 1 bids \(b\), it wins when every other bid is below \(b\), that is, when every other value is below \(\beta^{-1}(b)\). Each other value is below \(z\) with probability \(z\), and they are independent, so

\[ \Pr(\text{win}\mid b)=\big(\beta^{-1}(b)\big)^{n-1},\qquad \text{expected utility}=(v-b)\,\big(\beta^{-1}(b)\big)^{n-1}. \]

Step 2. The "pretend" trick

Rather than choosing a bid \(b\), let bidder 1 choose which value \(z\) to pretend to have, and bid \(\beta(z)\). Its utility is

\[ U(z;v)=\big(v-\beta(z)\big)\,z^{\,n-1}. \]

If \(\beta\) is an equilibrium, pretending to be yourself must be optimal: \(z=v\) maximizes \(U(\cdot\,;v)\). So the derivative in \(z\) vanishes at \(z=v\).

Step 3. First-order condition, then solve the ODE

\[ \frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\Big|_{z=v}=-\beta'(v)\,v^{n-1}+\big(v-\beta(v)\big)(n-1)v^{n-2}=0 . \]

Multiply by \(v\) and rearrange: \(\beta'(v)\,v^{n-1}+(n-1)\beta(v)\,v^{n-2}=(n-1)v^{n-1}\). The left side is exactly \(\frac{d}{dv}\big[\beta(v)\,v^{n-1}\big]\), so

\[ \frac{d}{dv}\big[\beta(v)\,v^{n-1}\big]=(n-1)v^{n-1} \ \Rightarrow\ \beta(v)\,v^{n-1}=\frac{n-1}{n}v^{n} \ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\ \beta(v)=\frac{n-1}{n}\,v\ } . \]

Step 4. Check it, and read it

Check (\(n=2\), so \(\beta(v)=v/2\)). A bidder with \(v=0.8\) whose opponent bids half its value wins with probability \(\min(2b,1)\):

Reading. \(\frac{n-1}{n}v\) is the expected highest other value given that it is below \(v\). That is, you bid what you would expect to pay in a second-price auction, conditional on winning. Shading shrinks as competition grows: half your value with 2 bidders, 90% with 10.

Revenue. The seller gets the highest bid, \(\frac{n-1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\max_j V_j]=\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}=\frac{n-1}{n+1}\). That is exactly second price's revenue, \(\mathbb{E}[\text{second-highest value}]=\frac{n-1}{n+1}\). This is not a coincidence; it is revenue equivalence (Lesson 4).

3.3 When there is no closed form: equilibrium within a class, and damped best response (12 min)

For MIRT-GSP or NGA there is no ODE to solve. The practical approach has two parts: restrict the bidding functions to a small family, and iterate best responses. We learn it on first price, where we know the answer is \(a^*=\tfrac12\) for \(n=2\).

Step 1. Restrict the class and define equilibrium in it

Take linear shading, \(\beta_a(v)=a\,v\). Write \(U(a'\mid a)\) for the expected utility of a bidder who uses \(a'\) while everyone else uses \(a\).

Symmetric equilibrium in a class, and deviation gain. \(a^*\) is a symmetric equilibrium in the class if no bidder gains by switching to another member of the class when the others play \(a^*\): \(a^*\in\arg\max_{a'}U(a'\mid a^*)\). The deviation gain measures the distance from equilibrium: \[ \mathrm{DG}(a)=\frac{\max_{a'}U(a'\mid a)}{U(a\mid a)}-1\ \ \ge 0,\qquad \mathrm{DG}(a^*)=0 . \] Note this is an equilibrium in the class: a bidder might gain by leaving the class. The Stackelberg papers check this with a richer family (PWL) after the fact.

Step 2. Compute \(U(a'\mid a)\) for first price, \(n=2\)

A bidder with value \(v\) bids \(a'v\) and beats the opponent when \(a'v>aV_1\), i.e. \(V_1\lt a'v/a\). If \(a'\le a\), that probability is \(a'v/a\) for every \(v\le1\), and

\[ U(a'\mid a)=\int_0^1 (1-a')v\cdot\frac{a'v}{a}\,dv=\frac{a'(1-a')}{3a}\qquad(a'\le a). \]

For \(a'>a\) the win probability caps at 1 when \(v>a/a'\). Splitting the integral there gives \(U(a'\mid a)=(1-a')\big(\tfrac12-\tfrac{a^2}{6a'^2}\big)\).

Step 3. The best-response map

\(\mathrm{BR}(a)=\arg\max_{a'}U(a'\mid a)\). When \(a\ge\tfrac12\) the maximizer of \(a'(1-a')\) is \(a'=\tfrac12\le a\), so \(\mathrm{BR}(a)=\tfrac12\). When \(a\lt\tfrac12\), the second formula gives a best response between \(a\) and \(\tfrac12\) (for example \(\mathrm{BR}(0.2)=0.284\) and \(\mathrm{BR}(0.4)=0.437\)). The fixed point \(\mathrm{BR}(a^*)=a^*\) is \(a^*=\tfrac12\), which matches 3.2.

Deviation gain at \(a=0.6\): \(U(0.6\mid0.6)=0.1333\), \(U(0.5\mid0.6)=0.1389\), so \(\mathrm{DG}(0.6)=4.2\%\). If everyone shades to 60%, each bidder would gain 4.2% by shading to 50%.

Step 4. Damped best response

\[ a_{t+1}=\tfrac12\,a_t+\tfrac12\,\mathrm{BR}(a_t)\qquad(\text{start at truthful } a_0=1). \]

Averaging the current profile with the best response is the standard fix, and it is exactly what the EC paper uses for CGA: \(h_{t+1}=\tfrac12h_t+\tfrac12\mathrm{BR}(h_t)\).

3.4 GSP: full-information vs Bayesian equilibria (10 min)

Return to the example of Lesson 2, 2.8: two slots with CTRs \(0.2\) and \(0.1\), three advertisers with values \(10, 8, 5\). Truthful GSP charges 8 and 5 per click (revenue \(0.2\cdot8+0.1\cdot5=2.1\)), but it is not an equilibrium, because advertiser 1 prefers the second slot.

Full information (Edelman–Ostrovsky–Schwarz 2007; Varian 2007)

GSP has many full-information Nash equilibria. The standard refinement is envy-free: no advertiser would rather have the slot above at the price the advertiser above pays. Among envy-free equilibria, the one with the lowest revenue charges exactly the VCG prices; every other one charges more. In the example, bids \((10,\ 6.5,\ 5)\):

  • Advertiser 1: top slot at 6.5 per click, so \(u=(10-6.5)\cdot0.2=0.7\). Dropping to slot 2 at 5 gives \((10-5)\cdot 0.1=0.5\). It stays.
  • Advertiser 2: slot 2 at 5, so \(u=(8-5)\cdot0.1=0.3\). Slot 1 at 6.5 would give \((8-6.5)\cdot0.2=0.3\). It is indifferent, which is where the lowest envy-free equilibrium sits.
  • Advertiser 3 bids its value and gets nothing; taking slot 2 would cost 6.5, more than its value.

Revenue: \(0.2\cdot6.5+0.1\cdot5=\mathbf{1.8}\), equal to VCG and below truthful GSP's 2.1. At equilibrium, GSP's extra revenue at truthful bids can vanish. Your NEK_GSP numbers show the same direction (ACOC: 3.62 truthful → 2.41 after response, Myerson 2.04). Balanced bidding is the dynamic that targets this equilibrium (Cary et al. 2007), when it converges.

Bayesian GSP

With incomplete information the picture changes. A symmetric BNE of GSP that ranks bidders by value would allocate efficiently. By revenue equivalence (Lesson 4) it would earn exactly VCG revenue. But Gomes and Sweeney (2014) show that such an equilibrium can fail to exist for some CTR profiles (check their conditions before citing). In MIRT the slates have externalities and organic content, so there is no closed form. You compute equilibria numerically in a class (3.3), which is exactly the engine of 3.7.

3.5 The seller leads over an equilibrium (10 min)

Now the Stackelberg view. The seller commits to a design \(\theta\): a reserve, \(\gamma\), or a regime such as ACOC or AUOF. The bidders respond, but their response is not one action. It is a whole equilibrium \(\beta^*(\theta)\). That gives a bilevel problem with an equilibrium as its inner level:

\[ \max_\theta\ \mathrm{Rev}\big(\theta,\ \beta^*(\theta)\big)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad \beta^*(\theta)\ \text{is a BNE of the mechanism } M_\theta . \]

The reaction function of Lesson 1 is now the equilibrium map \(\theta\mapsto\beta^*(\theta)\). For a DSIC mechanism the revelation principle makes it trivial (\(\beta^*=\mathrm{id}\)). For GSP or first price you must compute it.

Worked example: first price with a reserve \(r\), \(n=2\), \(U[0,1]\)

Step 1, the equilibrium map. A bidder with \(v\lt r\) cannot win. A bidder with \(v\ge r\) solves the same pretend problem as in 3.2, \(U(z;v)=(v-\beta(z))\,z\), with first-order condition \(\frac{d}{dv}[\beta(v)v]=v\). The boundary condition is new: the lowest participating type bids the reserve, \(\beta(r)=r\). Integrating from \(r\):

\[ \beta(v)\,v=\frac{v^2-r^2}{2}+r^2\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \beta^*_r(v)=\frac{v^2+r^2}{2v}\qquad(v\ge r). \]

The bidders' strategy depends on the seller's choice. At \(r=0.5\), a bidder with value \(0.8\) bids \(0.556\) instead of \(0.4\).

Step 2, revenue along the map. The highest value \(m\) has density \(2m\), and the winner pays \(\beta^*_r(m)\) when \(m\ge r\):

\[ \mathrm{Rev}(r)=\int_r^1\frac{m^2+r^2}{2m}\,2m\,dm=\frac13+r^2-\frac43r^3,\qquad \mathrm{Rev}'(r)=2r-4r^2=0\ \Rightarrow\ r^*=\tfrac12 . \]

This is the frame for GSP+MIRT Replace "reserve" with \(\theta=(\text{regime},\gamma)\) and "first price" with MIRT-GSP. There is no closed-form \(\beta^*(\theta)\), so compute it with 3.3. Then NEK's question ("is GSP revenue monotone in degrees of freedom?") becomes a comparison of \(\mathrm{Rev}(\theta,\beta^*(\theta))\) across regimes at equilibrium, not at truthful bids and not after full-information responses. Optimizing \(\gamma\) over it is the bilevel problem above, solved with ES (Lesson 1, 2.6, case C). No repeated-auction modeling is needed.

3.6 Uniform shading: invisible to rank-by-bid, visible to MIR (5 min)

Suppose every bidder multiplies its bid by the same \(a\).

  • Rank by bid (or bid × CTR): GSP, VCG, first price without reserve. The ranking is unchanged, so the allocation is unchanged, and per-click prices (which are other bids) scale by \(a\). Uniform shading is a pure transfer from seller to bidders.
  • MIR. The selected slate maximizes advertiser value plus \(\gamma\) times user welfare. With every bid scaled by \(a\), \[ \arg\max_{s}\ \big[a\,B(s)+\gamma\,W(s)\big]=\arg\max_s\ \Big[B(s)+\frac{\gamma}{a}\,W(s)\Big], \] so shading by \(a\) acts like raising \(\gamma\) to \(\gamma/a\): the slates tilt toward organic content. In MIRT-GSP, equilibrium shading changes the allocation and welfare, not just prices.

This is also the seed of the NGA argument in Lesson 6. If a learned mechanism is scale-equivariant and a single bidder barely moves what is learned, a deviator facing an all-\(a\) profile has the same incentives as at \(a=1\). So truthful bidding is the natural symmetric equilibrium, and departures from it measure the mechanism's one-shot regret.

3.7 The equilibrium engine: a recipe (5 min)

  1. Class. Choose a bidding family: linear \(a v\) first, then PWL (the papers use 9 knots).
  2. Deviation evaluation. For the current profile \(a\), simulate auctions in which everyone else bids \(\beta_a\). Estimate \(U(a'\mid a)\) for a grid of \(a'\) (or ES directions), using common random numbers: the same sampled auctions for every \(a'\), so that differences between deviations are not drowned by sampling noise.
  3. Update. \(a\leftarrow\tfrac12a+\tfrac12\mathrm{BR}(a)\). Stop when \(\mathrm{DG}(a)\) is below tolerance.
  4. Verify. From the final profile, search a richer class for a profitable deviation.
  5. Report. Revenue, welfare, advertiser utility and user welfare at \(\beta^*\), compared with truthful bidding and with VCG/Myerson.

For MIRT-GSP the mechanism stays fixed between iterations (its range never sees bids), so step 2 is pure simulation. For NGA the mechanism is retrained on the bids of each profile (Lesson 6), which makes every evaluation expensive and noisy and is why paired seeds matter.

Summary

  • Without truthfulness, predict bids with a Bayes–Nash equilibrium of bidding functions: each value's bid is optimal on average over the others' values. Full-information Nash (balanced bidding) is an upper bound on strategic gains.
  • First price, \(n\) bidders, \(U[0,1]\): \(\beta(v)=\frac{n-1}{n}v\), from the pretend trick plus an ODE. Its revenue equals second price's.
  • Without closed forms: an equilibrium within a class, the deviation gain, and damped best response \(a\leftarrow\frac12a+\frac12\mathrm{BR}(a)\).
  • GSP: the lowest full-information envy-free equilibrium earns exactly VCG revenue, so the extra revenue at truthful bids can vanish. Bayesian symmetric equilibria may not exist.
  • Seller leads: \(\max_\theta \mathrm{Rev}(\theta,\beta^*(\theta))\), optimizing along the equilibrium map. For first price with a reserve, \(r^*=\tfrac12\) and revenue goes from \(\tfrac13\) to \(\tfrac5{12}\).
  • Uniform shading is a pure transfer under rank-by-bid, but under MIR it acts like \(\gamma\to\gamma/a\).

Next. Lesson 4 (Myerson) explains why first and second price earn the same revenue, why \(r^*=\tfrac12\), and gives the time-change theorem for NEK's theory task.

Quiz

Try each one on paper before opening the answer.

Q1. First price with \(n=3\) bidders, values \(U[0,1]\). What is the symmetric equilibrium bid, and the seller's expected revenue?

\(\beta(v)=\tfrac23v\), and revenue is \(\frac{n-1}{n+1}=\tfrac12\), the same as second price.

Q2. In the derivation of 3.2, why may we assume \(z=v\) is optimal and set the derivative to zero there?

Because we are looking for an equilibrium. If \(\beta\) is a BNE, bidding \(\beta(v)\), that is "pretending to be \(v\)", is optimal by definition. So \(z=v\) maximizes \(U(z;v)\), and an interior maximum has zero derivative. The resulting ODE is a necessary condition. Checking sufficiency, as the table in Step 4 does for one value, completes the argument.

Q3. Compute the deviation gain at \(a=0.8\) in the linear first-price example (\(n=2\)).

\(\mathrm{BR}(0.8)=0.5\). \(U(0.5\mid0.8)=\frac{0.25}{2.4}=0.1042\) and \(U(0.8\mid0.8)=\frac{0.16}{2.4}=0.0667\). DG \(=0.1042/0.0667-1\approx 56\%\). The further from equilibrium, the larger the gain from deviating.

Q4. Run damped best response from \(a_0=0.2\) for two steps, using \(\mathrm{BR}(0.2)=0.284\) and \(\mathrm{BR}(0.242)\approx0.320\).

\(a_1=\tfrac12(0.2+0.284)=0.242\) and \(a_2=\tfrac12(0.242+0.320)\approx0.281\). The iterates climb toward \(\tfrac12\) from below (the left dots in Figure 2).

Q5. In the GSP example of 3.4, are the bids \((10,\ 7,\ 5)\) an envy-free equilibrium? What is the revenue?

Advertiser 1: top slot at 7 gives \(0.6\); slot 2 at 5 gives \(0.5\). It stays. Advertiser 2: slot 2 at 5 gives \(0.3\); slot 1 at 7 would give \((8-7)\cdot0.2=0.2\lt0.3\), so no envy. Advertiser 3 cannot profitably take slot 2. So yes, and revenue is \(0.2\cdot7+0.1\cdot5=1.9\), between VCG (1.8) and truthful GSP (2.1). There is a range of envy-free equilibria, and 6.5 is its bottom.

Q6. First price with reserve \(r=0.5\), \(n=2\). What does a bidder with value 0.6 bid? With value 0.4?

\(\beta^*(0.6)=\frac{0.36+0.25}{1.2}\approx0.508\), just above the reserve. With value 0.4 it cannot profitably win (it is below the reserve), so it does not participate, or bids anything below 0.5.

Q7. Why can't the seller in 3.5 choose \(r\) by assuming bidders keep bidding \(v/2\)?

Because the equilibrium moves with \(r\): \(\beta^*_r(v)=\frac{v^2+r^2}{2v}\), and higher reserves push bids up. Holding bids fixed means optimizing with the reaction term dropped, the Nash-style mistake of Lesson 1 (2.6). The leader must optimize along the reaction, here the equilibrium map.

Q8. MIR with \(\gamma=2\). Every advertiser shades to \(a=0.5\). What effective \(\gamma\) does the selection use, and which way do the slates move?

\(\gamma/a=4\). The user-welfare term weighs twice as much relative to bids, so the slates move toward organic and user-favored content.

Q9. In the engine of 3.7, what goes wrong if each deviation \(a'\) is evaluated on fresh random auctions?

The differences \(U(a'\mid a)-U(a''\mid a)\) are small compared with auction-to-auction noise, so the estimated best response jumps around. Common random numbers (the same auctions for every \(a'\)) cancel the shared noise and leave only the effect of the deviation.
