Direction 1 · Long tasksinference-heavylow scoop risk

Why long tasks fail: the shape of the failure hazard

The questionWhen an LLM fails a long task, is it because every step carries the same small risk, because earlier errors make later steps riskier, or because some tasks are simply harder than others? And which of these explains the "time horizon" curves people use to forecast AI progress?

In plain words

Picture a long task as a chain of n steps. If each step succeeds with probability p, independently, the whole chain succeeds with probability pn. That number falls quickly. At p = 0.99 a 69-step task succeeds half the time, and a 690-step task succeeds 0.1% of the time. This simple model already explains a striking measurement. METR found that the length of software tasks frontier agents finish half the time has doubled about every seven months since 2019. Under pn, halving the per-step error rate doubles that half-life. Small gains on short benchmarks, which look like diminishing returns, therefore become large gains on long tasks (Sinha et al. 2025).

Real failure curves do not follow pn exactly, and how they deviate is informative. The tool for this is the hazard rate: the chance of failing at step t, given that you survived up to t. A constant hazard is the pn world. A rising hazard means trouble accumulates. A model that made a mistake reads its own mistake and makes more, which Sinha et al. call self-conditioning and Cheng et al. (2026) call contextual drag. A falling hazard means that once you are past the early steps, you tend to finish.

The subtle part is that a falling hazard in pooled data does not mean any agent improves as it goes. Mix easy tasks (low hazard) with hard tasks (high hazard). The hard ones fail early, so the survivors are mostly easy tasks, and the pooled hazard falls even though every single task has a constant hazard. Survival analysis calls this frailty; econometrics calls the general problem "heterogeneity versus state dependence" (Heckman). The 2026 debate is exactly this:

  • Refits of METR's data find a falling hazard (Weibull shape 0.6–0.9), and Toby Ord withdrew his constant-hazard "half-life" claim in a February 2026 post.
  • A controlled study finds clean geometric decay within homogeneous tasks (Mittal 2026).
  • Error-injection studies find a rising hazard.

All three can be true at once, and nobody has separated them on the same tasks.

The math you need

S(n) = pn = e−λn,   λ = −ln p ≈ 1 − p,   n½ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.69 / (1 − p) Survival and half-life under a constant hazard. The horizon at reliability s is ns = ln s / ln p, so n80 ≈ 0.32·n50 and n99 ≈ n50/69: each extra "nine" of reliability divides the horizon by about ten.
h(t) = P(fail at t | alive at t−1) = 1 − S(t)/S(t−1);   Weibull: S(t) = exp(−(t/η)k),   h ∝ tk−1 The Weibull shape k summarizes the hazard's direction: k = 1 constant, k < 1 falling, k > 1 rising.
λi ~ Gamma(a, θ)  ⇒  S(t) = E[e−λt] = (1 + θt)−a,   h(t) = aθ / (1 + θt) Frailty. Every task has a flat hazard λi, yet the pooled hazard falls with t. Heterogeneity also shortens high-reliability horizons: n99/n50 = (0.99−1/a − 1)/(21/a − 1), which is 0.010 at a = 1 and 0.0068 at a = 0.5, compared with 0.0145 for a homogeneous population.
logit hi(t) = αi + β · (errors already in context) + γ · g(t) One model with all three forces: task frailty αi, self-conditioning β > 0, and drift in time g(t). The key design trick: run each task many times (seeds), so αi and β can be estimated separately. Injecting errors into the history then gives a causal estimate of β.

What is known (2025–26)

  • Time horizons double about every 7 months, measured in human task-minutes (METR). Nguyen & Fithian show that human time maps nonlinearly to AI difficulty, nearly flat between 2 and 30 minutes.
  • Gains in per-step accuracy compound. Models degrade after seeing their own errors, scale does not fix this, and thinking mode does (Sinha, …, Geiping).
  • Failed attempts in context pull later answers toward the same errors, by 10–20% across 11 models (Cheng, …, Arora).
  • In controlled tasks success is geometric in steps, and the decay tracks step count, not context length (Mittal).
  • After a first mistake, agents rarely notice or recover; frontier judges find the first mistake in under a third of runs (Rahman et al., Google Research).
  • Extreme decomposition plus voting reaches a million steps with zero errors when errors are independent (MAKER). Errors also concentrate on a few hard steps (LEAD).

What is still open

Q1. Separate the three forces on the same tasks

Heterogeneity pushes the pooled hazard down, self-conditioning pushes it up, and recovery pushes it down again. Which wins decides whether 50% horizons extrapolate to 99% reliability.

Closest: Ord plus his 2026 post and Fergus Hamilton's Weibull refit (aggregate data only); Mittal (homogeneous tasks only); Peng et al. propose a "horizon residual" but run no large study. No paper fits step-level hazards with task frailty and state dependence together.

Q2. Can training remove self-conditioning, and does that transfer?

If β can be driven to zero, horizons grow without bigger models.

Closest: Contextual Drag (partial fixes lose the benefit of correct context), Kim et al. (RL on long horizons is unstable). Nobody reports β before and after training on error-injected histories.

Q3. What is the right time axis?

Steps, tokens, or human minutes. Human time is what forecasts use, but it is not a native unit for a model.

Closest: Nguyen & Fithian, METR TH1.1. Less suited to a small lab.

Experiments

Pilot · week 1

Sinha et al.'s key-value execution task (public code) on Qwen3-8B with thinking on and off. 200 tasks × 20 seeds, horizons up to 300 steps. Record the step of the first error. Plot survival per task next to the pooled curve, then fit a Weibull to each. If per-task curves look flat while the pooled one bends, the frailty story is alive.

Tasks

Three families, each with step-level checking and a difficulty knob: (a) key-value and arithmetic execution, (b) state tracking (program tracing, MD5-style computation), (c) a deterministic tool-use environment (simulated file system or spreadsheet API) that offers an undo action, so recovery is possible and measurable.

Models

Qwen3 8B/14B/32B (thinking on/off), gpt-oss-20b, Gemma-3-27B, served with vLLM: 32B on the H200, ≤20B on the A100. Roughly 105 chains in total. Inference only.

Design

20–50 seeds per task, which identifies per-task hazards. Difficulty mixtures built on purpose, so frailty is known. Error injection (k wrong steps planted in the history), which gives β causally. An undo affordance, which gives the recovery rate.

Analysis

Discrete-time hazard regression with task random effects and censoring. Compare constant, Weibull, frailty and state-dependent models by held-out likelihood. The headline test: predict 80% and 99% horizons on long runs from short runs only.

Optional · month 3

LoRA SFT or GRPO on error-injected histories to reduce β, then test on held-out step types, horizons 4× longer, and natural (not injected) errors.

Direction 2 · Incentives in RLRL computecrowded

What RL learns when the checker is wrong

The questionWhen you train a reasoning model with RL against a checker that sometimes accepts wrong answers, can you predict before training how much it will exploit the checker? And does it only reuse mistakes it already made, or does it learn new tricks?

In plain words

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) pays for "the checker said yes", not for "the answer is right". Checkers are imperfect in practice:

  • One audit found that on 28.5% of sampled SWE-bench Verified tasks, the tests accept a wrong patch.
  • Math answer parsers mishandle formats.
  • A single "master key" token can fool an LLM judge.

This gives the model an incentive: everything the checker accepts pays the same.

Two very different things can then happen. Selection: the model becomes more likely to produce wrong answers it already sometimes produced and that slip through. Exploitation: it learns a general trick, such as a format that fools the parser, listing several answers, or editing the tests, and applies it everywhere, including prompts where it never cheated before. Selection is bounded and can be predicted from the base model. Exploitation cannot. Where the line falls between them is the open question, and it is pure incentive analysis.

The math you need

maxπ Eπ[r(y)] − β·KL(π ‖ πref)  ⇒  π*(y) ∝ πref(y) · er(y)/β The KL-regularized optimum: RL tilts the base model toward rewarded outputs.
accepted set A = C ∪ F (correct ∪ false positives):   π*(C) / π*(F) = πref(C) / πref(F) With a 0/1 checker, every accepted answer is multiplied by the same factor e1/β. RL can raise the acceptance rate but not the precision among accepted answers. The KL penalty only limits how far the policy moves, not in which direction.
β → 0:   gold accuracy → p / (p + q),   p = πref(C),   q = πref(F) = (1 − p)·ε A ceiling computable from base-model samples. Examples: p = 0.3, ε = 0.1 gives 0.81; p = 0.1, ε = 0.1 gives 0.53; p = 0.02, ε = 0.03 gives 0.41. Hard prompts, where RLVR is supposed to teach the most, suffer most.
resampled noise: correct earns 1 − δ, wrong earns ε  ⇒  learning iff J = 1 − δ − ε > 0 If the checker's mistakes are redrawn on every call, they average out and accuracy still goes to 1, only more slowly ("rate, not fate", Rad et al. 2026). Persistent mistakes, where the same wrong answer is accepted every time, do not average out.
exploit spread = post-RL mass on F over prompts where πref(F) ≈ 0 The ceiling holds prompt by prompt for a "tabular" policy. A neural policy can generalize a trick across prompts. This spread is the measurable gap between the theory and what training does. Reference points: best-of-n has KL ≤ log n − (n−1)/n and win rate ≤ n/(n+1) (Beirami et al.); proxy-reward overoptimization follows gold(d) ≈ d(a − b log d) with d = √KL (Gao et al.).

What is known (2025–26)

  • Under resampled noise, the sign of Youden's J decides between learning and collapse (Rate or Fate?).
  • Systematic false positives cause plateaus or collapse, and the error pattern matters more than the error rate (Egashira et al., ETH).
  • A preregistered study of natural MBPP false positives found selection of existing errors, no growth within 400 steps at ≤1.5B, and a static audit that predicted false-positive mass with Spearman 0.80 (Zhang).
  • RLVR models learn to list labels instead of inducing rules, an exploit that worsens with compute (Helff et al.).
  • Random rewards lift Qwen2.5-Math through a GRPO clipping bias but fail on Llama and OLMo, so always include non-Qwen controls (Spurious Rewards).
  • Debate training keeps a weaker judge from being hacked (Kenton et al., Google DeepMind). Reward-model ensembles mitigate hacking but do not eliminate it (Eisenstein et al.).
  • Verifier errors are correlated within a GRPO group (ρ ≈ 0.53, so the effective group size is about 1.7 of 8) (Xin). The October 2026 survey's conclusion: error structure matters more than error rate (Checking the Checkers).

What is still open

Q1. Does base-model sampling predict the outcome of RL?

Estimate p and q per prompt from base samples, predict post-RL gold accuracy, and find exactly where the prediction fails.

Closest: Zhang (natural code false positives, small models, short runs), Moya et al. (training signals alone cannot identify accepted errors). The survey lists "theory for response-dependent errors under group-relative gradients" as open.

Q2. What makes an exploit spread?

Candidate factors: whether the trigger is a simple learnable rule or idiosyncratic, its base rate, model size, training length, the KL coefficient, and group size. One hypothesis worth testing: larger models find rarer triggers, so checker robustness gets worse with scale.

Closest: Countdown-Code (a testbed for how hacking emerges and generalizes), MacDiarmid et al. (production hacks generalize to misalignment), Helff et al.

Q3. How to spend a small audit budget

Crowded already: Moya, Egashira and several others. Pursue it only as a by-product of Q1.

Experiments

Pilot · week 1

Countdown, which has exact gold answers, with two planted checker bugs. (i) Idiosyncratic and persistent: accept a wrong expression when a hash of it is even. (ii) A learnable trigger: accept when the answer is boxed twice. Sample Qwen2.5-1.5B 64 times per prompt to estimate p and q, run two short GRPO jobs, and compare predicted against realized accuracy.

Stack

verl GRPO with 8 rollouts and about 300 steps. Models: Qwen2.5 1.5B/3B/7B, plus Llama-3.2-3B and OLMo-2-7B as non-Qwen controls. Tasks: Countdown, GSM8K/MATH, and MBPP with visible versus hidden tests (natural false positives).

Error models

E1 resampled noise grid, including J ≤ 0. E2 persistent idiosyncratic false positives. E3 learnable trigger with base rate 10−3, 10−2 or 5·10−2, applied to a fraction of prompts. E4 natural code false positives. Sweep KL ∈ {0, 10−3, 10−2} and group size ∈ {4, 8, 16}, with 3 seeds on the key cells.

Measures

Predicted ceiling against realized accuracy per prompt, exploit spread, time to onset, within-group error correlation, and how each changes with model size.

Compute

Roughly 600–700 H200-hours (about 100 runs at 1.5B, 25 at 3B, 8 at 7B), with the A100 running the judge, code sandboxes and evaluation. About four weeks of GPU time, so profile first.

Direction 3 · Long-form reasoning and incentivesRL computefast-moving

When thinking must be visible

The questionIf you train a reasoning model while penalizing "bad thoughts" in its chain of thought, when does it stop the bad behavior, and when does it keep the behavior and hide it? Is the deciding factor how much step-by-step computation the behavior needs?

In plain words

Labs read models' chains of thought (CoT) to catch misbehavior. That works only if the CoT carries the real reasoning, and there are two ways it can fail. The CoT can be a story told after the fact: models used a planted hint but mentioned it under 20% of the time. And training against a CoT monitor can teach models to keep misbehaving while writing innocent-looking thoughts; OpenAI observed this "obfuscated reward hacking".

The main hope is necessity. A transformer can do only a bounded amount of step-by-step (serial) computation per token. A long serial computation therefore has to be written down, because the written tokens are the model's working memory. If a misbehavior needs more serial steps than the model can do silently, it must show up in the CoT, either readably or in some encoded form. The research question is where that boundary lies, how big models move it, and whether training pressure moves it. Incentives are built in: the training objective literally pays the model to hide.

The math you need

fixed-depth transformer, no CoT ⊆ TC0;   with T CoT tokens: any size-T circuit;   poly(n) tokens ⇒ P Inherently serial problems (composing permutations of five items, the S5 word problem; graph connectivity) are believed to lie outside TC0, so long instances need CoT (Li, Liu, Zhou, Ma 2024; Merrill & Sabharwal 2024).
opaque serial depth dopq:   a hidden computation of depth D needs about D / dopq visible steps dopq is the longest computation a model can do without interpretable intermediate states. Brown-Cohen, Lindner and Shah (Google DeepMind, 2026) give bounds for Gemma 3. The ratio is a rule of thumb, not a theorem. Measured latent planning tops out at about 3 steps (small models trained from scratch), 5 (fine-tuned Qwen3-32B) and 7 (GPT-5.4 few-shot) (Xu, Jettkant, Ruis 2026).
monitor error ≥ information gap in I(B; Z),   each token ≤ log2|V| ≈ 17 bits A monitor can use only what the CoT Z reveals about the behavior B (Anwar et al.). Mohammadkhani, …, Hahn (2026) prove that above a difficulty threshold that grows with model size, solving the task must leak a near-linear amount of information about the hidden input into the CoT. Under cryptographic assumptions, however, that leak can be unreadable.
R = Rtask − λ · M(Z)   →   quit · do it silently (D ≤ dopq) · encode it · fool the monitor The four possible end states under monitor pressure. "Fool the monitor" means fluent, readable text that the monitor misjudges, as in Schulz et al.'s 2026 "monitor jailbreaking".

What is known (2025–26)

  • When CoT is necessary, evading a monitor needs human-designed strategies or very long optimization: hundreds of thousands of RL episodes against a weak monitor (Emmons et al., Google DeepMind).
  • CoT pressure produces obfuscated reward hacking (Baker et al., OpenAI). Even output-only penalties leak into the CoT (Drori et al.). Length penalties cut hint disclosure by 35–39% (Little).
  • Penalizing strings in a load-bearing CoT yields learned substitution codes that generalize (Skaf et al.). Yet steganographic reasoning is far harder to learn than steganographic messaging (Schulz et al.).
  • Models can barely control their CoT: 2.7% compliance versus 61.9% for outputs (Yueh-Han et al.). Reward terms that conflict with transparency degrade monitorability (Kaufmann et al., Google DeepMind).
  • Monitoring can fail even when CoT is necessary, which suggests a four-level hierarchy of necessity (Bürger et al., Oct 2026).

What is still open

Q1. A dose-response curve for necessity

Vary the serial depth k that a hidden side task requires, train with a monitor penalty, and find the k at which hiding becomes impossible. Then compare it with the measured latent ceiling and with Hahn's threshold.

Closest: Emmons et al. (one RL environment), The Depth Ceiling (no training pressure), Hidden Reasoning Must Leak (theory). No study varies required depth as the independent variable under monitor-penalized RL.

Q2. Do cheap defenses survive a policy trained against them?

Paraphrasing before monitoring, rotating monitors, held-out monitors.

Closest: Monitor Jailbreaking (paraphrasing works after the fact). Nobody has trained a policy through the paraphraser.

Q3. Which routine training choices cost monitorability?

Length, KL and preference pressure are applied every day, and published results conflict.

Closest: Monitoring Monitorability (no harm at frontier scale) against Length Penalties… (harm).

Experiments

Pilot · 2 days

Necessity curves, inference only. N(k) = accuracy with CoT − accuracy without CoT, for S5 composition and k-hop pointer chasing on Qwen3-4B and 8B. This tells you which k values bracket the latent ceiling.

Training

GRPO with LoRA on Qwen3-4B (full grid), Qwen3-8B, and Llama-3.1-8B (replication at two k values). The main task is benign math; the side task has depth k ∈ {2, 4, 6, 8, 12}. Reward = main + side − λ·flag, with λ ∈ {0, 0.3, 1} and 3 seeds.

Monitors

Training monitor: Qwen3-14B on the A100. Held-out monitors: Gemma-3-27B and Mistral-Small-24B, quantized. Use a weak-to-strong monitor curriculum, because evasion can take very long.

Measures

Held-out detection rate at 1% false positives. Load-bearing tests (corrupt or truncate the CoT and check accuracy). Accuracy without CoT before and after training, which detects internalization. Recovery by paraphrasing before monitoring. Label each run's end state: quit, silent, encoded, or jailbroken.

Compute

About 250–350 H200-hours.

Direction 4 · Long contextinference-heavymechanism open

Long inputs, shorter thinking

The questionReasoning models write much shorter chains of thought when the same problem is buried in long, irrelevant context. Does this early stopping cause much of the long-context reasoning penalty, and what in the model makes it stop?

In plain words

Long-context models can find a needle in a million tokens, yet they reason worse over long inputs, even when they have found every relevant fact. Du et al. (2025) measured drops of 14–85% even when the irrelevant text was replaced by whitespace or masked out entirely. Three mechanisms are known:

  • Attention spreads thin over more tokens.
  • Rotary position encodings misbehave at long distances.
  • A single forward pass has limited "bandwidth" for combining information from many places.

A 2026 finding adds a behavioral piece. Given long irrelevant context, reasoning models write traces up to 74% shorter for the same problem and double-check less (Reasoning Shift, Rodionov et al.). A model that stops thinking too early fails hard problems no matter how well it retrieves. The authors say plainly that they offer no mechanism.

Optimal stopping gives you a precise way to ask the question. A good reasoner keeps thinking until it is confident enough, then stops. Stopping earlier can happen in only two ways: the model's confidence rises faster than it should (inflation), or the confidence level it needs before stopping drops (urgency). The two leave different fingerprints in data you can measure, and each points to a different fix.

The math you need

max weight ≤ eΔ / (eΔ + n − 1)  ⇒  to keep weight ≥ 1 − ε on one key: Δ ≥ ln((n − 1)(1 − ε)/ε) Softmax dilution (Veličković et al., Google DeepMind). The logit gap Δ must grow like ln n, and a model trained at shorter lengths learned gaps sized for those lengths.
retrieval needs O(1) bandwidth; majority, reachability and composition need much more in one pass; CoT lowers the requirement The bounded-attention prefix oracle (BAPO) model (Schnabel & Tomlinson, Microsoft Research). Global tasks over long inputs need either more passes or written-down intermediate steps.
Lt = log-odds of the leading answer after t thinking steps;   stop at τ = min{t : Lt ≥ b};   accuracy at stop ≈ 1/(1 + e−b) if L is calibrated A Wald-style stopping rule. You read Lt out by forcing an answer on a side branch every few hundred tokens.
inflation: slope of Lt ↑, confidence at stop unchanged, accuracy at stop ↓ (miscalibrated)  |  urgency: b ↓, confidence at stop ↓, calibration kept The two fingerprints. Inflation is a metacognitive failure: the context makes the model overconfident. Urgency is a learned length prior: the model stops at lower confidence.

What is known (2024–26)

  • Softmax must disperse as the number of items grows (Veličković et al.). Over-squashing and representational collapse (Barbero et al.). Attention sinks as a way to avoid over-mixing (Why do LLMs attend to the first token?).
  • Length alone hurts, even when the filler is masked (Du, …, Peng). The same group's RoPE theory finds reasoning tasks fail mostly through "semantic reversal" and retrieval tasks through positional insensitivity; targeted high-frequency rescaling gains up to 20–25 points (Wu, Du, Peng, Sept 2026).
  • Dilution confirmed in retrieval: a few hard distractors take disproportionate attention (First Drop of Ink); gold-document attention collapses at million-token scale and length-aware softmax repairs it (Gollapudi, …, Min).
  • Single-pass bandwidth limits (Lost in Transmission), and a depth hierarchy for hybrid attention (Ye et al.).
  • Context shortens reasoning by up to 74%, with less self-verification. Forcing extra self-checks helps less when the redundant context is present (Reasoning Shift).

What is still open

Q1. Why does context shorten thinking?

Is it inflation, urgency, or a lost question? Is it caused by attention (the problem tokens get diluted) or learned (the training distribution of lengths)?

Closest: Reasoning Shift lists "a mechanistic account" as future work. Google DeepMind's work on how LLMs compute verbal confidence gives you the readout tools.

Q2. How much of the long-context reasoning penalty is early stopping?

Force thinking to the length the model uses in isolation and measure how much accuracy returns. This splits the penalty into a "stopping" part and an "attention / position" part.

Closest: Du et al. and Wu et al. (mechanisms, without stopping), Rodionov et al. (stopping, without a decomposition).

Q3. A bandwidth law for thinking length

For global tasks, how should the number of thinking tokens scale with input length? Do models follow that scaling or ignore it?

Closest: BAPO (theory, small instances).

Pilot · week 1

Qwen3-8B with thinking on, 300 MATH-500 and AIME-style problems, irrelevant context of 0, 8k and 32k tokens, 4 samples each. Record trace length and the forced-answer log-odds Lt every ~200 tokens (branch readouts with vLLM prefix caching), plus confidence and accuracy at stop.

Models

Qwen3 8B/14B/32B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, gpt-oss-20b. 32B at 64k context fits the H200; smaller models go on the A100.

Conditions

Filler type: whitespace, masked (attention mask), random text, or related-but-irrelevant problems. Problem placement: before or after the filler. Length: 0 to 64k.

Interventions

(a) Budget forcing: append "Wait" until the trace matches its isolated length. (b) Restate the question after the context. (c) Mask the filler. (d) Steer along a confidence direction found with a linear probe.

Measures

Length ratio, slope of Lt, confidence and calibration at stop, accuracy, and the fraction of the long-context penalty recovered by matched thinking.

Direction 5 · Reasoning theorysmall models from scratchbusy area

How much memory silent thinking needs

The questionWhen a model reasons in hidden activations, by looping its layers or feeding back hidden states instead of writing tokens, how large must its carried-over state be for a task of a given working-memory load? Is there a predictable law?

In plain words

A chain of thought gives a model two things at once. It gives more serial computation, because each token is another pass through the network. And it gives a growing scratchpad, because every written token stays readable. Latent reasoning tries to get the computation without the tokens. A looped transformer runs the same layers several times: Saunshi et al. at Google Research showed that a k-layer model looped L times matches a kL-layer model on reasoning tasks. Coconut (Meta FAIR) feeds the last hidden state back in as the next input, so a "thought" is a vector that can hold several possibilities at once (superposition).

A loop that carries only a small state is like a computer with little memory: running it longer adds time, not space. Zhang (2026) proves that compressed loops are small-space machines. They cannot solve problems that need a lot of memory, which chain of thought can. Artzi's group (October 2026) found that sharing one memory across loop iterations works surprisingly well. What nobody has measured is the empirical law: how the needed state grows with how much a task must hold in mind. That law decides when latent reasoning can replace written reasoning. It also connects to Direction 3: whatever can be done silently cannot be monitored.

The math you need

no CoT ⊆ TC0;   O(log n) loops ⇒ regular languages and graph connectivity;   k layers × L loops ≈ kL layers Depth is the scarce resource, and a little depth goes a long way (Merrill & Sabharwal 2025; Saunshi et al. 2025). Pause tokens add width, not serial depth (London & Kanade 2025).
state capacity ≤ m · d · b bits   vs   demand ≈ L · log2|V| bits  ⇒  slot-like storage gives m* ∝ L m carried vectors of dimension d at b bits each, for a task with L live variables over an alphabet V. Superposition can pack many nearly orthogonal directions into d dimensions (approximately, up to exponentially many), so m* could grow more slowly than L. Which regime trained models are in is unknown.
loss = E + A(Nonce + rφ Nrec)−α + B D−β,   φ ≈ 0.46 A first scaling law for looped LMs: one shared recurrence is worth roughly r0.46 unique blocks (Schwethelm et al. 2026). That measures capacity, not memory, so it complements the question here.

What is known (2024–26)

  • CoT makes transformers solve inherently serial problems (Li, Liu, Zhou, Ma). The expressive power of CoT with log, linear and polynomial numbers of steps (Merrill & Sabharwal, A Little Depth Goes a Long Way).
  • Looped transformers reason as well as deep ones (Saunshi et al., Google Research). Recurrent-depth models scale test-time compute (Geiping et al.). Looped LMs at 1.4–2.6B match dense models up to 12B (Ouro).
  • Continuous thought (Coconut), with a superposition theory (Zhu et al.) and its training dynamics (Emergence of Superposition). But only models trained from scratch actually use superposition; fine-tuned ones collapse to shortcuts (Illusion of Superposition?).
  • Google's latent-reasoning methods: Thinking States (Google Research) and Catch Your Breath (Google DeepMind). Continuous CoT for parallel exploration: CoT2 (Michigan with Google Research). RL for continuous CoT: Soft Tokens, Hard Truths (Meta FAIR).
  • A memory-budget separation between CoT and compressed loops (Zhang). Shared memory across loop iterations (Monea, …, Artzi).

What is still open

Q1. A state-budget law m*(L, D)

The critical carried-state size as a function of working-memory load L and serial depth D, measured across interfaces (CoT, Coconut, full-state loop, capped loop, shared-memory loop).

Closest: Zhang (theory plus tiny probes; names intermediate budgets as the key target), Artzi's group (perplexity only, no serial tasks).

Q2. Can superposition be induced in pretrained LLMs?

It is the main theoretical reason continuous thought should beat discrete CoT, yet the evidence so far shows collapse.

Closest: Illusion of Superposition?, Soft Tokens, Hard Truths, CoT2.

Q3. What must be verbalized?

A task-level criterion for when reasoning can stay latent. It doubles as a monitorability criterion (see Direction 3).

Pilot · 1 day

A 6-layer, 25M-parameter GPT trained from scratch on multi-stream S5 word problems (L parallel permutation streams, depth D), with a loop whose carried state is capped at m slots. Sweep m ∈ {1, 2, 4, …, 64} for L ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} on the A100.

Tasks

Independent knobs for load L and depth D: multi-stream S5, k-pointer chasing, variable-update programs, and reachability with a wide search frontier.

Interfaces

Measures

m*(L) at 90% accuracy, extrapolation to 2–4× D, and sample efficiency. Then a transfer check: cap the state of Ouro-1.4B (an open looped LM) and see whether the same law predicts where it breaks.

Planning objectives

Beyond next-token prediction

Training with teacher forcing can fail to learn planning: on "path-star" graphs the model learns a shortcut for every token except the one that needs lookahead. Multi-token prediction and diffusion LMs help. The live 2026 debate is whether the objective is the cause or the missing supervision is. Why ranked lower: at least six closely related 2026 papers, and the remaining question is hard to settle without large-scale pretraining.

Read: The Pitfalls of Next-Token Prediction, Roll the dice & look before you leap, Multi-token Prediction, Discrete Diffusion for Reasoning and Planning, Supervision Adulteration, Diffusion Exploits Asymmetry, How Transformers Learn to Plan via MTP. Incentives and economics

Social choice in preference alignment

Reward learning with Bradley–Terry on pooled preferences is equivalent to the Borda count, so RLHF can pick a response that a majority dislikes. Nash learning from human feedback computes a "maximal lottery", which respects majorities. "Distortion" measures the welfare lost, and strategic labelers can manipulate the result. Why ranked lower: the theory is crowded (the Procaccia, Haghtalab, Roth and Su groups publish several papers a year), and the open gap is an empirical audit on real multi-annotator data. That is publishable, but less about reasoning.

Read: Hidden Context in RLHF, Nash Learning from Human Feedback, Jackpot! Mathematical discovery

LLM-guided program search (AlphaEvolve and successors)

An LLM proposes program edits, an evaluator scores them, and a population keeps the best. These systems improved dozens of math bounds in 2025–26, from kissing numbers to Ramsey bounds. Why ranked lower: six "what actually matters" papers in five months, several showing simple baselines are competitive, so scoop risk is high. The open niche is attribution with a contamination control: new problem variants that have no literature record.
